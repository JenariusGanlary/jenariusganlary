You're not confused because you're not smart enough. You're confused because nobody's shown you the first actual step.

You want to build your own thing. You've known it for a while. But every time you sit down to start, you hit the same wall: where do I even begin?

People tell you to build an audience. Pick a niche. "Just start." Nobody tells you what starting actually looks like on a Tuesday, after work, when you have forty minutes and no idea what to do with them.

So you watch one more video. Save one more thread. And stay exactly where you are — busy, but not moving.

## The confusion isn't the problem — it's the symptom

Here's what nobody says out loud: the confusion isn't the problem. It's the result. It's what happens when you're handed the end state — launch something, get customers, go viral — without ever being shown the first rung of the ladder.

I run into this constantly with my own side projects. I have a full-time MIS and data analyst job, and everything I build — CreatorBit, freelance work through Ganlary Labs — happens in the hours around that job. When I'm tired and the idea is still shapeless, "build an audience" is useless advice. It's a destination, not a direction. What I actually need on a Tuesday night is a task so small I can't talk myself out of it.

That's the gap most build-in-public advice leaves open. It optimizes for the six-month picture and skips the five-minute one.

You don't need more information. You need a first step small enough that you'll actually do it.

## What the first step actually looks like

So here it is, stripped down to four moves:

1. **Pick one small thing to build.** Not a startup. One project, one service, one product.
2. **Define who it's for in one sentence.** Not everyone. One person whose problem you understand.
3. **Set a deadline so short it feels silly.** Two weeks, not six months.
4. **Start today.** Write one honest line about it in public.

None of these steps require permission, a plan, or a niche. That's the point. Each one is small enough to finish in an evening, which means the only thing standing between you and step one is deciding to do it.

The order matters too. Most people try to reverse it — find the audience first, then figure out what to build for them. That's backwards for a first project. You don't have enough signal yet to know what an audience wants; you have enough signal to know one problem you personally understand. Start there.

## Why "bad" is the correct first version

Your first step won't make money. It won't impress anyone. It'll probably be bad.

That's fine — bad is a version that exists, and it's a hundred times closer to free than the perfect idea you never started. A rough landing page that says what you're building and why is worth more than a polished idea sitting in your notes app, because the rough version can be reacted to, corrected, or ignored — and any of those three outcomes teaches you something the polished idea in your head never will.

This is the part that trips people up when they come from technical backgrounds. I did, too. Engineers are trained to ship something correct. Building in public asks you to ship something honest instead, before it's correct — a distinction that feels uncomfortable the first few times and stops feeling uncomfortable after about the fifth.

## What "starting" actually means when you have a day job

If you're building alongside full-time work, the constraint isn't motivation — it's that every plan has to survive contact with a day that's already full. That changes what "start today" has to mean in practice.

It doesn't mean quitting your job, blocking off a weekend, or waiting for a slow week that never comes. It means treating the four-step list above as something you can execute in the gaps: the one-sentence audience definition on your commute, the two-week deadline set during lunch, the first honest public post written in the twenty minutes after dinner instead of scrolled past. I've written before about what that actually looks like day to day in [How I Build a SaaS While Working a Full-Time Job](/blog/building-a-saas-while-working-full-time) — the short version is that the schedule gets smaller, not the ambition.

The deadline being short isn't arbitrary — it's what makes the plan survive a full-time job. A six-month goal competes with everything else in your life and loses. A two-week goal fits inside the cracks of a normal week, which means it's the only kind of goal that actually gets finished by someone who isn't building full-time yet.

## The clarity comes from beginning, not before it

I'm not a guru who figured it all out. I'm in the middle of this myself: building while holding down a job, shipping in public, one step ahead of you at best.

What I can tell you with more confidence is this: the clarity you're waiting for before you start doesn't arrive before you begin. It comes from beginning. Every project I've actually finished became clearer in week two than it was in week zero, not because I thought about it more, but because I had something real to react to.

Start with the small thing. Write the honest line. The rest — the audience, the direction, the version two — sorts itself out from there, but only after there's a version one to sort out from.

What's the one small thing you've been meaning to build? I'd genuinely like to know — it's usually more specific than people think once they say it out loud.

## Frequently asked questions

**What does "building in public" actually mean if I have nothing built yet?**
It means sharing the process before there's a finished product to show — the decision to start, the first rough version, the mistakes along the way — rather than waiting until something is polished enough to be proud of. The public part is what creates the accountability that gets the private part finished.

**Do I need an audience before I start building in public?**
No. Trying to build an audience before you have anything to say to it is backwards — you don't yet know what that audience would want, but you do know one problem you understand. Start by building for one specific person, and the audience forms around the honest documentation of that process, not the other way around.

**How small should my first project actually be?**
Small enough to finish in about two weeks, working around a full-time job or other commitments. If the deadline feels almost embarrassingly short, that's usually a sign it's scoped correctly — a goal that competes with the rest of your week for six months rarely survives it.

**What if my first version is genuinely bad?**
That's expected, not a warning sign. A bad version that exists can be reacted to, critiqued, and improved; a perfect idea that stays in your head can't. Most people who eventually ship something good started with a first version they'd now be a little embarrassed by.
